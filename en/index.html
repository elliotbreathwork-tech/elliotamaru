01Breath and the brain

Breath, a dialogue with the brain.

Recent research shows that the rhythm of our breathing is directly linked to our brain activity.

Researchers have observed a synchronization between the breathing cycle and the activity of different brain regions, including those involved in emotions, attention and certain cognitive functions.

When we are stressed or on alert, our breathing tends to speed up. Conversely, slowing the breath on purpose can help move our physiological state toward more calm and recovery.

Breathwork invites us to explore this relationship between breath, body, brain and consciousness.

Through different breathing techniques, we experience first-hand what our breathing can change in our physical and inner state.

It is about learning to listen to the breath, to play with its rhythms, to develop a finer awareness of the body and to discover how breathing can become a real tool for presence and regulation.

This is what I share here.

Five chapters to follow the breath, from the lungs to the nervous system.

02The lungs

Blood is already saturated with oxygen.

97 to 98% at rest. Breathing harder leaves that figure unchanged.

480 million

alveoli, about 70 m² for exchange

What decides how much oxygen your brain and muscles receive is CO2: where cells produce it, hemoglobin lets go of its oxygen.

If you breathe too fast, you blow off that CO2 and the oxygen stays attached. Your head spins.

03The diaphragm

The diaphragm opens the lungs.

You breathe 20,000 times a day. With every inhale, this dome of muscle, stretched like a sail, moves down, your rib cage expands and air rushes in.

At rest, it moves down about 2 cm. In deep breathing, 5 to 7 cm: more than double, and the air coming in follows.

On the exhale it rises back up on its own. That return, you can slow down.

The vagus nerve runs through the middle of this muscle, the thread that links your breath to your heart.

04The vagus nerve

Six breaths per minute. At this rhythm, your heart changes its behavior.

The heart speeds up when you inhale and slows down when you exhale. That gap, heart rate variability, peaks at six breaths per minute.

This process runs through the vagus nerve, which links the brainstem to the heart, the lungs and the gut.

4 out of 5

of the vagus nerve fibers travel from the body up to the brain

A hunch often arrives this way.

Lengthen your exhale: the vagus nerve engages the brake and the heart slows within a second. It is the only voluntary lever you have on the autonomic nervous system.

05The belly

You have more neurons in your belly than in your spinal cord.

Between 200 and 600 million, in your intestines. They form the enteric nervous system, which works on its own.

9 out of 10

of the vagal fibers in the abdomen carry information up to the brain

Your breath acts directly on these neurons. Belly breathing is tied to your emotions.

Taoist medicine knew this long before the laboratories: for it, the belly is the center of the body, where tensions and feelings accumulate.

Two ways to soften it: breath from the inside, the hand from the outside.

06The nervous system

Breath acts on the whole nervous system.

The nervous system connects everything you have just seen: it starts in the brain and reaches, through the spinal cord, the tips of your fingers and toes.

3 times around the Earth

the length of your brain's nerve fibers, laid end to end

Two branches share the control: the sympathetic, the accelerator, and the parasympathetic, the brake, of which the vagus nerve is part. Your state in the moment comes from their balance.

Fast, deep breathing followed by a pause raises adrenaline. Slow breathing, under ten cycles per minute, calms and reduces anxiety and anger.

In a session, you go through both: the surge, then the calm.

  3.   4.   5. About

Elliot Amaru: Meeting our inner state (@theinnerstatelab (opens in a new tab))

What if the path to ourselves began with a breath, a silence, or an encounter able to overturn our view of the world?

My name is Elliot Amaru. For seven years, my story has taken me across continents, to meet age-old traditions and to learn from people whose knowledge is passed down far beyond diplomas and books.

Holistic therapist, Hatha Yoga teacher, Chi Nei Tsang practitioner and facilitator of ancestral ceremonies, I have gradually woven an approach that links the body, the breath and the subtler dimensions of human experience.

From breath to self-knowledge

My path led me to the foot of the Himalayas, where I completed 300 hours of training in conscious connected breathing with Mike O'Meara, founder of Evolution of Breath (opens in a new tab) and a major figure in the field today. I also took four weeks of online training in the Wim Hof method, 112 hours of training in Chinese medicine and Chi Nei Tsang with Hendrik van der Merwe, and 200 hours of training in Hatha Yoga and pranayama at Mind Flow Harmony, in Rishikesh.

Each of these practices explores a different way in: the breath and its effects on our inner state, movement and presence, or listening to the body through touch.

A path that is not learned only in books

At 24, a shamanic experience profoundly changed my life. It opened a door to a path of exploration and inner understanding that I never would have imagined possible.

That quest then took me to the heart of the Amazon and to the Sacred Valley of Peru, where I deepened my bond with ancestral traditions. Through months of traditional plant diets, lived in retreat and introspection, I undertook deep work on myself: opening my heart, sharpening my intuition and going to meet my most buried wounds, with the intention of understanding them, soothing them and, at last, freeing myself from them.

It was by going through this process myself that I gradually found my place among the tribes I had come to discover. Once this path was complete, I had the privilege of assisting many shamans in their ceremonies and learning at their side. These experiences nourished my understanding of accompaniment and, over time, led me to create my own ceremonial spaces.

This path taught me that the art of holding space is not about bringing all the answers, but about creating the conditions for each person to explore their own.

The Inner State Lab: a space to explore your inner world

Today, I bring these experiences together in a multidisciplinary approach that combines the science of breath, Eastern wisdom and spiritual traditions.

Over the course of my journey, I have guided more than 2,500 people, in individual sessions, in groups and on retreats, with the privilege of witnessing many realizations, inner healings and essential transformations.

Through breathwork, yoga, Chi Nei Tsang and ancestral medicines, I offer and create a setting where slowing down, listening, feeling and reconnecting with oneself becomes possible.

I share these practices with respect, humility and discernment, without imposing any truth or ready-made path.

The Inner State Lab is an invitation to explore what lies beyond the noise of everyday life, to meet one's own depths and to cultivate a more conscious relationship with oneself, with others and with all living things. With a spirit of non-judgment, acceptance and compassion.
