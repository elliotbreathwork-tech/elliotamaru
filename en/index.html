01Breath and the brain

Breath, a dialogue with the brain.

Recent research shows that the rhythm of our breathing is directly linked to our brain activity.

Researchers have observed a synchronization between the breathing cycle and the activity of different brain regions, including those involved in emotions, attention and certain cognitive functions.

When we are stressed or on alert, our breathing tends to speed up. Conversely, slowing the breath on purpose can help move our physiological state toward more calm and recovery.

Breathwork invites us to explore this relationship between breath, body, brain and consciousness.

Through different breathing techniques, we experience first-hand what our breathing can change in our physical and inner state.

It is about learning to listen to the breath, to play with its rhythms, to develop a finer awareness of the body and to discover how breathing can become a real tool for presence and regulation.

This is what I share here.

02The lungs

Your blood is already saturated with oxygen.

At rest, saturation reaches 97 or 98%. Breathing harder leaves that level unchanged. What decides how much oxygen your muscles and brain receive is carbon dioxide. Christian Bohr described this mechanism in 1904. It carries his name, and it explains why breath training works in the opposite direction from what you'd expect.

480 million

alveoli, about 70 m² for exchange

In these alveoli, oxygen attaches to the hemoglobin in your red blood cells. It boards in the lungs and gets off in the tissues. Boarding is the easy part: everything is decided at the drop-off.

CO2 is what makes it get off. When your cells work, they produce carbon dioxide. This local acidity loosens hemoglobin's grip, and it lets go of its oxygen right where it is needed.

When you breathe too much, you drive that CO2 out. Hemoglobin closes around its cargo and keeps it. Your lungs fill with air, your brain receives less oxygen. Hence the dizziness when the rhythm runs away.

CO2 controls the release of oxygen, and your tolerance of this gas can be trained. A trained person handles levels that set off panic in someone else. That is a good part of what we do during the session.

03The diaphragm

Your diaphragm opens your lungs.

A lung inflates in the space your rib cage gives it. That volume comes from the diaphragm, a dome of muscle stretched under the lungs like a sail. With each inhale, it drops, the rib cage expands and air rushes in.

At rest, the diaphragm drops about 2 cm. In deep breathing, it travels 5 to 7 cm, more than double, and the air coming in follows.

Exhaling takes little effort. The diaphragm relaxes, rises, and the air leaves. You can slow down that return.

The vagus nerve passes through the middle of this muscle, running down from the brain to the belly. It is what links your breath to your heart, the subject of the next chapter.

04The vagus nerve

Six breaths per minute. At this rhythm, your heart changes its behavior.

Your heart beats at a rhythm that varies: it speeds up when you inhale and slows down when you exhale. This gap is called heart rate variability, and it measures the state of your autonomic nervous system. At six breaths per minute, this gap reaches its maximum. Paul Lehrer and Richard Gevirtz named this point the resonance frequency.

This gap runs through the vagus nerve. It starts at your brainstem, runs down the neck, wraps around the heart, brushes the lungs and goes deep into the gut. It touches most of your organs.

4 out of 5

of the vagus nerve fibers travel from the body up to the brain

Your head receives more information from your organs than it sends them. A hunch often arrives this way.

In the other direction, the vagus nerve carries orders, and your exhale activates the branch that brakes. Lengthen it, and your heart slows with it, within the second. It is the only voluntary lever you have on your autonomic nervous system.

05The belly

You have more neurons in your belly than in your spinal cord.

Your gut holds between 200 and 600 million neurons. This network is called the enteric nervous system, and it works on its own.

This network decides alone. It moves the contents of the gut along, regulates local blood flow, adjusts secretions. Cut the vagus nerve, and it carries on.

9 out of 10

of the vagal fibers in the abdomen run from the belly up to the brain

Your breath reaches this network, and breathing into the belly is also tied to your emotions. Taoist medicine discovered this long before the laboratories: for it, the belly is the center of the body, where tensions and feelings accumulate.

The diaphragm presses on this area with every breath. When the breath stays stuck high up, the belly loses that movement and stiffens in turn.

Two paths to give that movement back. Breath, from the inside. The hand, from the outside. We work on it in session.

06The nervous system

Your breath acts on your whole nervous system.

Everything you have just seen is connected by a single network, your nervous system. It starts in the brain and reaches, through the spinal cord, the tips of your fingers and toes. Its autonomic part runs your heart and your digestion for you.

3 times around

the Earth: the length of your brain's nerve fibers, laid end to end

This autonomic system has two main branches. The sympathetic speeds up the heart and opens the airways. The parasympathetic slows the heart and restarts digestion. The vagus nerve, which you met earlier, belongs to this second branch.

Most of your organs, including the heart and the gut, receive fibers from both branches. They most often act in opposite directions, and your state at any moment comes from their balance.

Your breath speaks to both. Fast, deep breathing followed by a pause raises adrenaline: volunteers trained in this technique showed it in 2014. Slow breathing, under ten cycles per minute, calms: a review of fifteen studies finds more relaxation and alertness, and less anxiety and anger.

Five minutes a day for a month is the dose tested in a trial of 108 adults: breathing with a long exhale improved mood and lowered resting breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation did.

In a session, you go through both: the surge of connected breathing, then the calm that follows.

Frequently asked questions

What people ask me before a first session

What is a private breathwork session like?

Four stages. Introduction: a moment to get acquainted and set down what brings you here and what weighs on you. Warm-up: movement, heart coherence, Qi Gong, pranayamas. Guided breathing: sharing and practice of conscious connected breathing techniques. Return to normal: we come back gently to our natural state, then talk through the experience. All of it set to music, with playlists I put together myself over the sessions.

Do I need to have practiced before?

Everyone is welcome. I adapt to your level, whether you are discovering breathwork or have practiced it for a long time.

Does breathwork heal anything?

Yes, it heals, and its benefits are real. Slow, conscious breathing calms the nervous system: stress goes down and sleep deepens. The body lets go of its tension. The people I guide talk about lightness and clarity when they leave a session, and studies on slow breathing point the same way. It is a wellness practice: it goes alongside your medical care, and diagnosis remains the doctors' business. Talk to your doctor before practicing if you are pregnant, take medication for the heart or blood pressure, or live with epilepsy, glaucoma, poorly controlled asthma or a recent operation.
